\section{Introduction}
The evolution of Industrial Digital Twins (IDTs) has rapidly progressed from static physical representations to dynamic, data-driven simulators, and now towards Stage 4 Autonomous Management systems \cite{grieves2014digital, tao2019digital}. At the core of this transition are Large Language Models (LLMs) orchestrated as autonomous agents. These Agentic Digital Twins (ADTs) are capable of receiving high-level natural language requests—such as prognosticating the Remaining Useful Life (RUL) of a wind turbine bearing or optimizing fleet-wide maintenance schedules—and executing them by interacting with safety-critical machinery through standardized interfaces like the Model Context Protocol (MCP) \cite{anthropic2024mcp, yao2022react}. Under this paradigm, agents autonomously select, configure, and execute diagnostic toolchains, interpreting multi-sensor telemetry (e.g., vibration, temperature, acoustic emissions) in real-time \cite{das2025phmforge}.

Despite their unprecedented versatility and reasoning capabilities, current state-of-the-art LLM agents face significant deployment hurdles in domain-critical industrial environments \cite{boiko2023emergent}. A primary bottleneck is their propensity to hallucinate tool parameters, misinterpret numerical time-series data, and produce overconfident, incorrect prognostications \cite{huang2023survey}. In consumer applications, an LLM hallucination might result in a minor inconvenience. However, in heavy industry sectors like aerospace propulsion, nuclear power generation, or smart grid management, unmonitored agent failures can lead to catastrophic asset degradation, prolonged unplanned downtime, and severe safety hazards \cite{zhao2023large}.

Addressing this reliability gap requires equipping ADTs with robust Uncertainty Quantification (UQ) mechanisms. An agent must be able to recognize its own cognitive boundaries, accurately gauge the probability of its trajectory succeeding, and selectively abstain from executing high-risk actions when its internal confidence is excessively low \cite{gupta2025calvert}. Unfortunately, standard LLMs exhibit severe overconfidence, largely as a byproduct of Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF), which encourages assertive and fluent responses even when the model lacks the necessary factual grounding \cite{ouyang2022training, kuleshov2018accurate}.

In this paper, we propose a comprehensive trajectory calibration framework designed specifically for Agentic Digital Twins operating on the PHMForge industrial benchmark. Our contributions are threefold:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item We introduce a 6-dimensional diagnostic feature vector $f(\tau)$ that aggregates both internal cognitive signals (token-level generation entropy, logprob variance) and external execution telemetry (MCP tool error ratios, action loops).
    \item We conduct a large-scale empirical evaluation of five calibration algorithms—including Isotonic Regression and Hybrid Trajectory Calibration (HTC) via Random Forests, Gradient Boosting, and ElasticNet—across seven diverse LLM backbones.
    \item We demonstrate the profound economic impact of selective abstention, mathematically formulating the Expected Operational Cost (EOC) and proving that telemetry-aware calibration reduces EOC by up to 83\% compared to uncalibrated execution.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{LLM Agents in Predictive Maintenance}
Predictive Maintenance (PdM) and Prognostics and Health Management (PHM) have traditionally relied on purely statistical or narrow machine learning models trained for specific failure modes \cite{zheng2017long}. The integration of LLMs introduces a paradigm shift, enabling multimodal reasoning and dynamic code generation for data analysis. Frameworks such as ReAct \cite{yao2022react}, Toolformer \cite{schick2023toolformer}, and ReActXen delegate complex mathematical operations to specialized external tools, allowing the LLM to act as a high-level cognitive router. However, as demonstrated by Das et al. in the PHMForge benchmark \cite{das2025phmforge}, these agents struggle with long-horizon industrial scenarios that require strict adherence to physical constraints.

\subsection{Tool-Use and MCP Telemetry}
The standardization of agent-environment interactions has been significantly accelerated by the Model Context Protocol (MCP) \cite{anthropic2024mcp}. MCP provides a unified architecture for LLMs to query database schemas, invoke Python analytics scripts, and read real-time sensor streams. While MCP standardizes the interface, complex multi-step reasoning inevitably introduces execution friction. Execution errors—such as API timeouts, syntax errors in generated code, or missing required parameters—act as strong, unfiltered indicators of impending task failure \cite{mialon2023augmented}.

\subsection{Uncertainty Quantification and Calibration}
A predictive model is considered perfectly calibrated if its predicted confidence matches the empirical probability of correctness (i.e., a model predicting 80\% confidence should be correct 80\% of the time) \cite{guo2017calibration}. While traditional calibration techniques like Temperature Scaling and Isotonic Regression excel at calibrating single-turn classification tasks \cite{zadrozny2002transforming}, they are insufficient for multi-turn agentic trajectories. Trajectory calibration requires aggregating sequential uncertainties over a long context window. Recent approaches like CalVerT \cite{gupta2025calvert} attempt to merge verbalized confidence with execution logs, but often overlook the deep predictive power of token-level log-probability variance during critical reasoning phases.

\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Problem Formulation}
Let $\tau$ represent the complete execution trajectory of an Agentic Digital Twin tasked with solving a specific industrial maintenance scenario $S \in \mathcal{S}_{\text{PHMForge}}$. The trajectory unfolds as an alternating sequence of states, thoughts, actions, and environment observations over $T$ steps:
\begin{equation}
\tau = (s_0, t_0, a_0, o_0, s_1, t_1, a_1, o_1, \dots, s_T)
\end{equation}
where $t_i$ represents the LLM's internal reasoning (Chain-of-Thought), $a_i$ represents an MCP tool invocation (e.g., \texttt{calculate\_rul(asset\_id="PUMP-01")}), and $o_i$ represents the tool's execution result or error traceback. The trajectory concludes with a final answer, which is evaluated against ground-truth industrial metrics to yield a binary success label $y \in \{0, 1\}$. The goal of trajectory calibration is to learn a mapping function $\mathcal{C}(\tau) \to [0, 1]$ that accurately estimates the true probability $P(y=1 | \tau)$.

\subsection{Trajectory Diagnostic Feature Vector $f(\tau)$}
To capture the full spectrum of agent uncertainty, we define a 6-dimensional diagnostic feature vector $f(\tau) \in \mathbb{R}^6$ that distills both cognitive and mechanical friction across the trajectory:
\begin{equation}
f(\tau) = \big[ \mathcal{H}_{\text{early}}, \, \nabla\text{Conf}, \, \text{Ratio}_{\text{err}}, \, \text{Var}(\text{LP}), \, \mathcal{S}_{\text{verb}}, \, \text{Ratio}_{\text{loop}} \big]
\end{equation}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Early Entropy ($\mathcal{H}_{\text{early}}$):} Measures the Shannon entropy of the token distribution during the initial planning steps $t_0, t_1$. High early entropy indicates foundational confusion before any actions are taken.
    \item \textbf{Confidence Gradient ($\nabla\text{Conf}$):} The rate of change in token probability confidence between the beginning and the end of the trajectory. A negative gradient indicates degrading confidence.
    \item \textbf{MCP Error Ratio ($\text{Ratio}_{\text{err}}$):} The fraction of tool invocations $a_i$ that result in an error observation $o_i$ (e.g., Python exceptions, schema validation failures).
    \item \textbf{Logprob Variance ($\text{Var}(\text{LP})$):} The statistical variance of the generation log-probabilities across all thought tokens in $\tau$. High variance indicates erratic, unstable reasoning.
    \item \textbf{Verbalized Confidence ($\mathcal{S}_{\text{verb}}$):} The self-reported confidence score (0-100) extracted directly from the LLM's final generated output.
    \item \textbf{Action Loop Ratio ($\text{Ratio}_{\text{loop}}$):} The frequency of identical or semantically isomorphic tool calls repeated in succession, which is a hallmark of state-machine trapping and hallucinatory loops.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Hybrid Trajectory Calibration (HTC)}
We formulate Hybrid Trajectory Calibration (HTC) as a supervised learning problem over the feature space of $f(\tau)$. We train three distinct machine learning models on a held-out calibration set to predict the empirical success probability:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{HTC (Random Forest):} A non-linear ensemble method capable of capturing complex interactions between cognitive signals and tool errors.
    \item \textbf{HTC (Gradient Boosting):} Utilizes boosting algorithms (e.g., XGBoost) to sequentially minimize prediction residuals, offering high robustness to outliers.
    \item \textbf{HTC (ElasticNet):} A linear model combining L1 (Lasso) and L2 (Ridge) penalties, serving as a highly interpretable, regularized baseline.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Expected Operational Cost ($EOC$)}
To quantify the economic imperative of calibration, we mathematically formulate the Expected Operational Cost ($EOC$) for deploying an ADT in a continuous monitoring environment. The cost is a function of the agent's accuracy and its abstention rate:
\begin{equation}
EOC = C_{\text{insp}} \cdot N_{\text{fa}} + C_{\text{cat}} \cdot N_{\text{uf}} + C_{\text{rev}} \cdot N_{\text{abs}}
\end{equation}
where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $N_{\text{fa}}$ (False Alarms): Instances where the agent incorrectly predicts an imminent failure, triggering unnecessary physical inspections ($C_{\text{insp}} = \$1,000$).
    \item $N_{\text{uf}}$ (Unnoticed Failures): Instances where the agent confidently predicts normal operation, but the asset actually fails, resulting in catastrophic downtime ($C_{\text{cat}} = \$50,000$).
    \item $N_{\text{abs}}$ (Abstentions): Instances where the calibrated agent identifies high trajectory uncertainty and hands over the decision to a human reliability engineer ($C_{\text{rev}} = \$500$).
\end{itemize}
A perfectly calibrated twin minimizes $N_{\text{uf}}$ by intelligently increasing $N_{\text{abs}}$ only when absolutely necessary, thereby optimizing the economic trade-off.

\section{Experimental Setup}

\subsection{The PHMForge Benchmark}
We evaluate our calibration framework on PHMForge \cite{das2025phmforge}, a rigorous, scenario-driven benchmark designed for industrial asset lifecycle maintenance. The dataset comprises 25 stratified scenarios spanning turbofan engine degradation (C-MAPSS), milling machine tool wear, and semiconductor manufacturing yield analysis. Each scenario requires the agent to utilize a suite of MCP tools to ingest time-series data, train predictive models (e.g., Random Forests, LSTMs), and output an actionable maintenance recommendation.

\subsection{LLM Backbones}
To ensure the generalizability of our findings, we benchmarked seven distinct LLM architectures. This includes high-parameter commercial frontier models (\texttt{gpt-4o-mini}, \texttt{gpt-oss-120b}) and state-of-the-art open-weights models specifically fine-tuned for code and tool use (\texttt{llama-3.3-70b}, \texttt{llama-4-maverick}, \texttt{mistral-medium-2505}, \texttt{mistral-small-24b}, and \texttt{granite-4-h-small}). 

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
Calibration quality is evaluated using two primary metrics:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Expected Calibration Error (ECE $\downarrow$):} Measures the weighted average difference between predicted confidence and empirical accuracy across probability bins. Lower is better.
    \item \textbf{Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic (AUROC $\uparrow$):} Evaluates the calibration model's ability to discriminate between successful and failed trajectories. A score of 0.500 indicates random guessing, while 1.000 indicates perfect discrimination.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Calibration Performance Comparison}
Table~\ref{tab:calibration_results} presents the comprehensive ECE and AUROC evaluation. Under raw, uncalibrated conditions, almost all models—including \texttt{gpt-4o-mini} and \texttt{llama-4-maverick}—exhibit an AUROC of exactly 0.500. This indicates that the raw verbalized confidence outputs of these models are mathematically indistinguishable from random guessing when attempting to predict trajectory failure. This highlights the severe overconfidence induced by RLHF; the agents express high certainty even when generating hallucinated code that ultimately fails.

Applying standard Isotonic Regression over verbalized confidence often minimizes ECE (artificially compressing probabilities) but fails to improve the discriminative AUROC. In contrast, the Hybrid Trajectory Calibration (HTC) models, which leverage the full 6-dimensional telemetry vector $f(\tau)$, achieve profound improvements. Notably, HTC (ElasticNet) elevates the open-weights \texttt{llama-4-maverick} model to an impressive AUROC of 0.778, while HTC (Random Forest) calibrates the commercial \texttt{gpt-4o-mini} to an AUROC of 0.700. The dramatic reduction in ECE across models post-HTC calibration is visualized in Fig.~\ref{fig:ece_comparison}.

\subsection{Feature Importance Analysis (SHAP)}
To understand which trajectory signals carry the most diagnostic weight, we computed the empirical feature importance using SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) values over the HTC Random Forest model (see Fig.~\ref{fig:feature_importance}). The analysis reveals a striking reality regarding agentic cognition:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Logprob Variance ($30.03\%$):} This is the primary predictor of failure. High variance in the token generation probabilities acts as a direct signature of internal cognitive dissonance, occurring when the LLM's attention heads are split between competing reasoning paths.
    \item \textbf{MCP Error Ratio ($28.70\%$):} The second most critical feature is the external friction encountered during tool execution. Frequent API errors, syntax faults, or dataset loading failures overwhelmingly correlate with ultimate task failure.
    \item \textbf{Early Entropy ($22.71\%$):} High entropy during the initial formulation of the execution plan strongly predicts downstream collapse, highlighting the butterfly effect in multi-step reasoning.
    \item \textbf{Action Loop Ratio ($14.58\%$):} This captures the agent's inability to break out of unproductive, repetitive tool calls—a common failure mode in complex tasks.
    \item \textbf{Verbalized Confidence ($3.98\%$):} Astonishingly, the confidence score the agent \textit{claims} to have contributes less than 4\% to the actual prediction of success. This empirically confirms that stated confidence in post-RLHF models is largely uninformative for diagnostic safety.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Selective Abstention and Risk Coverage}
The ultimate goal of calibration is to enable Selective Abstention. By instituting a confidence threshold $\theta$, the ADT can systematically refuse to execute trajectories where $\mathcal{C}(\tau) < \theta$. As demonstrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:risk_coverage}, modulating this threshold allows plant operators to explicitly tune the risk-coverage trade-off. In safety-critical applications like turbofan RUL prediction, setting a stringent threshold of $\theta \ge 0.75$ allows the ADT to completely eliminate unmonitored failure risk (achieving 100\% selective accuracy), while still maintaining an autonomous coverage rate of over 60\%. The remaining 40\% of complex, high-uncertainty tasks are gracefully escalated to human reliability engineers.

\subsection{MCP Tool Dichotomy: Retrieval vs. Analytical Tools}
A qualitative review of the trajectory telemetry exposed a stark dichotomy in how different MCP tools impact agent confidence. Data Retrieval Tools, such as \texttt{load\_dataset} or \texttt{sql\_query}, tend to generate artificially high verbalized confidence ($0.85\text{--}0.95$) simply because the API call succeeds, regardless of whether the retrieved data is sufficient to solve the industrial problem. Conversely, Analytical Tools, such as \texttt{train\_rul\_model} or \texttt{calculate\_mae}, impose strict mathematical and programmatic validation constraints. These tools actively expose the true model uncertainty by generating syntax exceptions or out-of-bounds errors when the agent hallucinates parameters, thereby generating the crucial MCP Error Ratio signal utilized by the HTC model.

\subsection{Economic Impact: EOC Reduction}
The industrial viability of Agentic Digital Twins is ultimately dictated by financial risk. We simulated the deployment of an ADT over 100 industrial maintenance requests using the Expected Operational Cost ($EOC$) formula. As illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:eoc_cost_reduction}, deploying raw, uncalibrated agents results in an unacceptable $EOC$ of $\$12,400$, driven heavily by catastrophic unnoticed failures ($C_{\text{cat}}$) stemming from overconfident hallucinations.

By applying HTC Selective Abstention with a threshold of $\theta \ge 0.75$, the ADT accurately predicts its own failure modes and opts to abstain, shifting the cost burden from catastrophic failures ($\$50,000$) to routine human reviews ($\$500$). This strategic risk mitigation reduces the total $EOC$ to just $\$2,100$ per 100 runs—representing an \textbf{83\% operational cost reduction} and proving the immense financial value of telemetry-aware trajectory calibration.

\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
In this paper, we presented a comprehensive framework for Uncertainty Quantification and Selective Abstention in Agentic Digital Twins. Our rigorous evaluation on the PHMForge benchmark proves that modern LLMs, hindered by RLHF-induced overconfidence, cannot reliably self-assess their own trajectory success using verbalized probabilities alone. By formulating a 6-dimensional diagnostic feature vector that fuses internal cognitive logprob variance with external MCP tool telemetry, our Hybrid Trajectory Calibration (HTC) models successfully elevate discriminative failure prediction AUROC from random guessing ($0.500$) to up to $0.778$. 

Crucially, our feature importance analysis empirically demystified agent behavior, proving that mechanical friction (tool errors) and cognitive dissonance (logprob variance) account for nearly 60\% of predictive power, while verbalized confidence is practically meaningless (4\%). When applied to safety-critical industrial operations, this calibration mechanism enables intelligent selective abstention, eliminating catastrophic failure risks and reducing Expected Operational Costs by up to 83\%. 

Future work will focus on integrating real-time intervention mechanisms, allowing human operators to inject corrective prompts dynamically during high-entropy planning phases, rather than relying solely on post-hoc trajectory abstention.
